\begin{abstract}
Deploying face recognition on embedded systems demands models that balance biometric accuracy against strict constraints on latency, memory, and power. This chapter presents a systematic evaluation of vision transformer (ViT) architectures for low-resolution face identification, covering the full pipeline from architecture selection through compression to on-device inference. Starting from 16 model variants across six families---DeiT-3, Swin Transformer, PVT-v2, MobileViT-v2, ConvNeXt, and ResNet---we evaluate standard LoRA in preliminary sweeps, LoRA with progressive backbone unfreezing in final comparisons, feature-level knowledge distillation with two teacher configurations, structured pruning (head removal and block dropping), and post-training quantization (FP16, INT8) on the TinyFace benchmark. Over 100 resulting configurations are benchmarked on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano using TensorRT. Key findings include: (1) compact transformers outperform larger CNNs on low-resolution inputs---Swin-Tiny (27.5M parameters) with optimally tuned knowledge distillation ($\alpha{=}0.0$) achieves 0.616 rank-1 identification rate, exceeding ResNet-200 (62.6M) by $+$0.148; (2) dynamic INT8 reduces the four representative unpruned model files by $3.8$--$3.9\times$ with at most 0.0016 absolute rank-1 loss, while the largest additional loss across 24 pruned models is 0.0099; (3) block dropping improves Swin accuracy by up to $+$0.028 rank-1, confirming depth over-parameterization; and (4) out-of-the-box TensorRT conversion fails for ConvNeXt, PVT-v2, and MobileViT-v2 due to unsupported normalization and attention export patterns, but per-family module-level rewrites enable native TensorRT inference for all 16 architectures. A deployment decision guide summarizes the resulting trade-offs between recognition accuracy, native inference latency, memory, energy and engine size.
\keywords{Vision Transformers, Face Recognition, Edge AI, Model Compression, Knowledge Distillation, TensorRT}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vision transformers (ViTs) have emerged as an alternative to convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for computer vision, offering unique benefits especially relevant to embedded systems. Unlike CNNs, which extract features hierarchically via localized receptive fields, ViTs treat an image as a sequence of patches (tokens) and apply self-attention globally. This global receptive field enables ViTs to capture long-range dependencies and holistic context in an image, which can improve robustness for recognition under significant optical distortions or at long standoff distances~\cite{rodrigo2024transformersvsCNN}. For example, transformers can associate spatially distant image regions, potentially outperforming CNNs in scenarios where spatial relationships span the entire image. Modeling global relationships is advantageous in embedded vision applications like aerial imagery or remote biometric recognition, where important cues may be distributed across the frame.

However, ViTs initially came with drawbacks that posed challenges for resource-constrained devices. Early ViT models were extremely large with the Vision Transformer (ViT) model requiring hundreds of millions of parameters and pre-training on massive datasets to match CNN accuracy. For instance, the original ViT-Huge had over 600 million parameters and needed extensive training data (e.g. JFT-300M) to outperform CNNs~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}. At this scale, high model complexity and memory demands are impractical for embedded devices. Moreover, lacking the built-in inductive biases of CNNs (e.g. translation invariance and locality), vanilla transformers tend to require more data or regularization to generalize well~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}. With limited training data, a ViT can struggle to learn stable features, whereas CNNs’ local filtering and weight sharing act as useful constraints. These factors historically limited the adoption of ViTs for edge computing devices.

Despite these challenges, recent research demonstrates that vision transformers can be made efficient and scalable for edge deployment. Data-efficient training techniques and compact architectures have narrowed the gap between ViTs and CNNs on limited data~\cite{touvron2021deit,mehta2022mobilevit}. Properly 
designed transformer models can now achieve competitive accuracy and runtime on embedded hardware. EfficientFormer-L1 attains 79.2\% ImageNet top-1 accuracy with only 1.6~ms latency on an iPhone 12 (CoreML), matching the speed of a MobileNet-V2 while outperforming it by over 4\% in accuracy~\cite{li2022efficientformer}. This progress overturns the notion that transformers are too heavy for mobile/embedded applications. ViTs also offer an architectural flexibility (modular self-attention layers) that is amenable to model compression techniques (pruning, quantization, etc.) and parallel acceleration on modern neural processing units (NPUs)~\cite{liu2025survey_vit_edge,ceva2023transformer_npu}. In summary, vision transformers provide discriminative global image representations that are increasingly scalable, and ongoing innovations are making them viable for embedded systems. 

This chapter explores the development of ViTs for embedded systems in detail. Section~\ref{sec:anatomy} provides an overview of the ViT architecture including explanations of the core components and descriptions of ViT variants and other models explored in this work. Section~\ref{sec:optim} highlights optimization strategies commonly employed for training transformers including low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and knowledge distillation. Section~\ref{sec:experimental} provides an experimental setup that analyzes performance of the various ViT and CNN models through the lens of a challenging biometrics dataset, TinyFace. The TinyFace dataset features small facial crops, averaging $30 \times 30$ pixels, with facial imagery distorted by poor resolution and compression artifacts, motion blur, diverse illumination, and off-axis poses \cite{cheng2018lowresface}. This dataset was selected because it simulates the type of data that a real-world embedded system may process as part of a surveillance and facial recognition system. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:performance} presents and analyzes the biometric and computational performance metrics produced by the various models and optimization techniques on the TinyFace dataset. These results provide the reader with valuable data and insights for selecting the correct architecture for their application.

\section{Anatomy of Vision Transformers}\label{sec:anatomy}
The ViT architecture in Figure~\ref{fig:vit_arch} depicts two core components – patch and token generation and the transformer encoder. Both are examined in detail in the following subsections with additional exploration of the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) mechanism within the transformer encoder.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/vit_arch.png}
    \caption{High-level architecture of a Vision Transformer (ViT) from the original ViT paper from Dosovitskiy et al.~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}. An input image is divided into fixed-size patches, embedded into tokens, and processed by a stack of transformer encoder blocks composed of multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and feed-forward layers. The source diagram's label ``Multi-Head Attention'' denotes MHSA: queries, keys, and values are derived from the same token sequence.}
    \label{fig:vit_arch}
\end{figure}

At a high level, the ViT takes a $P \times P$ image and breaks it into $Q \times Q$ patches such that $\left(P/Q\right)^2$ patches are generated, e.g., a $224 \times 224$ image split into $16 \times 16$ patches generates $\left(224/16\right)^2=14^2=196$ patches. These patches are converted into token vectors and fed to the transformer encoder. The encoder performs self-attention between all patches to weight the vectors based on global ``importance" and then performs a non-linear transformation via multilayer perceptron (MLP), also referred to as feed forward network (FFN), to generate a new token representation that is fed to the next encoder layer. The encoder process is repeated through multiple encoder layers effectively refining the token representation before delivering the image representation to a task-specific head. This explanation skips over many crucial details that will be presented in the following sections but provides a clear picture of how images flow through the transformer model.

\subsection{Self-Attention and Multi-Head Mechanism}
The fundamental component of a transformer is the self-attention layer. Before attention is applied, an image is divided into patches and each patch is converted into a \emph{patch embedding}: a learned, fixed-length vector representation of that patch. For an RGB image, flattening a $Q\times Q$ patch produces a vector $\mathbf{p}_i$ of $3Q^2$ pixel values; a shared learned linear projection maps it to $\mathbf{e}_i=\mathbf{E}\mathbf{p}_i+\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$, where $\mathbf{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times3Q^2}$~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}.

For a small example, an $8\times8$ RGB image divided into $4\times4$ patches produces a $2\times2$ grid: top-left, top-right, bottom-left, and bottom-right. Each patch contains $4\cdot4\cdot3=48$ values. Projecting each to, for example, $D=12$ dimensions and listing them row by row yields a sequence $(\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2,\mathbf{e}_3,\mathbf{e}_4)$ of four 12-dimensional tokens. Positional embeddings identify their original grid locations (Section~2.3). In the DeiT-3 experiments here, $96\times96$ RGB inputs with $16\times16$ patches similarly yield a $6\times6$ grid, or 36 patch tokens, before adding the classification token.

Self-attention then computes interactions between every pair of these tokens. Specifically, each token $i$ is projected to a query vector $q_i$, key vector $k_i$, and value vector $v_i$ in some latent dimension. The attention operation compares queries and keys via scaled dot-products: $s_{ij} = q_i \cdot k_j / \sqrt{d_k}$, where $d_k$ is the query/key dimension and scales the attention scores. A softmax is then applied to $\{s_{ij}\}$ across all tokens $j$ to obtain attention weights $\alpha_{ij}$, which indicate how much token $i$ attends to token $j$. These weights are used to compute a weighted sum of the value vectors $v_j$ for token $i$, updating its representation. Thus, the full attention operation may be represented as
\begin{equation}
    \text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}
where $Q$, $K$, and $V$ are matrices containing the $q$, $k$, and $v$ vectors for all tokens, respectively. This allows information from one token to directly influence another, regardless of spatial distance, enabling the global receptive field of ViTs~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.

Rather than use a single attention head, transformers commonly employ multi-head self-attention (MHSA). Multi-head attention is the broader mechanism: in self-attention, queries, keys, and values come from the same sequence; in cross-attention, queries come from a different sequence than keys and values. The encoder attention described here is self-attention, and we use MHSA consistently for it. In MHSA, the token embeddings are projected into multiple subspaces via multiple linear layers (heads), and independent self-attention operations are performed in each subspace in parallel. After performing the parallel attentions, the lower-dimensional tokens are concatenated to produce a token of the same dimension as the original input token. Figure~\ref{fig:multihead_attention} provides an example where three heads are applied to three tokens of length 12, i.e., $d=12$.
\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{multihead_attention.png}
    \caption{Example of multi-head self-attention (MHSA) on three tokens of length 12 ($d=12$) where each token is passed to three separate linear projection heads to project the tokens to a smaller dimension (4). Thus, each head ideally learns a different representation of the token indicated by the varying shades. Self-attention is performed in parallel for each subspace and the resulting token vectors are concatenated to produce tokens of the same dimensionality as the original.}
    \label{fig:multihead_attention}
\end{figure}

In the example of Figure~\ref{fig:multihead_attention}, three heads are used and each head processes all three tokens of length 12 ($d=12$). Thus, the projected dimension is equal to the original token dimension ($d$) divided by the number of heads ($N_H$) such that $\frac{d}{N_H}=4$, as depicted by the smaller tokens to the right of the heads. The smaller tokens have varying shades of color to indicate that each head learns a different representation of the original token. Then, self-attention is performed independently within each subspace, i.e., there are three self-attention operations performed in this example. The output of the independent attention operations are concatenated to produce tokens with the same dimensionality as the original input token.

This design has two benefits: (1) it allows parallel computation of attention, improving throughput on hardware accelerators by performing many smaller matrix multiplies simultaneously; and (2) each head can learn attention in a different representation subspace, so the model can attend to different kinds of features or relationships in parallel~\cite{vaswani2017attention,zhang2021d2l}. In essence, MHSA enables more nuanced and diversified learning, as tokens are compared on distinct portions of their feature descriptors across heads. The outputs of all heads are concatenated and passed through a linear projection to form the final attended representation for each token.

\subsection{Transformer Encoder Structure}
A vision transformer is constructed by stacking multiple \emph{encoder blocks}, each composed of a multi-head self-attention (MHSA) sub-layer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (FFN), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vit_arch}. Modern ViT architectures typically adopt a \emph{pre-layer normalization (Pre-LN)} design to improve optimization stability, especially for deep models~\cite{xiong2020preln}. In this formulation, layer normalization is applied before both the attention and FFN sub-layers, and each sub-layer output is added to its input via a residual skip connection.

Layer normalization is used in place of batch normalization for several reasons. Unlike batch normalization, which normalizes activations across the batch dimension and therefore depends on batch statistics, layer normalization operates independently on each token by normalizing across feature dimensions. This makes layer normalization well suited to transformer architectures, where inputs are naturally represented as sequences of tokens and batch sizes may be small or variable. In embedded and low-latency settings, small batch sizes are common due to memory constraints, making batch normalization unstable or ineffective. Moreover, layer normalization is invariant to sequence length and batch size, simplifying deployment and enabling consistent behavior during both training and inference~\cite{ba2016layernorm}.

Formally, given an input token sequence $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$, where $N$ is the number of tokens and $D$ is the token length, an encoder block computes
\[
\mathbf{X}' = \mathbf{X} + \mathrm{MHSA}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{X})),
\]
\[
\mathbf{X}'' = \mathbf{X}' + \mathrm{FFN}(\mathrm{LN}(\mathbf{X}')),
\]
where $\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)$ denotes layer normalization, $\mathbf{X}'$ is the intermediate output of the residual MHSA block, and $\mathbf{X}''$ is the output token sequence passed to the next encoder block. This residual formulation preserves gradient flow and enables stable training as depth increases~\cite{he2016resnet}.

The FFN is applied independently to each token and consists of two linear transformations, $W_1$ and $W_2$, with a non-linear activation $\sigma$ in between:
\[
\mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = W_2 \, \sigma(W_1 \mathbf{x}),
\]
where $W_1$ expands the feature dimension, $W_2$ projects it back to the model dimension, and $\sigma=\frac{x}{2}\left( 1 + erf\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\right)$ is the Gaussian Error Linear Unit (GELU) activation function commonly used in ViT architecture. While the MHSA sub-layer performs global information mixing across tokens, the FFN provides local, channel-wise feature transformation~\cite{vaswani2017attention}.

By stacking $L$ encoder blocks (e.g., $L=12$ or $24$), the model incrementally refines token representations. Earlier layers tend to encode low-level visual patterns and coarse spatial relationships, whereas deeper layers capture higher-level semantic concepts through repeated global attention and nonlinear transformation. This hierarchical abstraction, achieved without explicit convolution, is a defining characteristic of vision transformers and underpins their strong representational capacity~\cite{dorszewski2025concepts_vit}.

\subsection{Tokenization and Position Embeddings}
Before entering the transformer encoder, an input image must be converted into a sequence of tokens. In the standard Vision Transformer (ViT) formulation, this tokenization is performed by partitioning the image into fixed-size, non-overlapping patches (e.g., $16\times16$ pixels) and linearly projecting each flattened patch into a $D$-dimensional embedding space, typically achieved via convolutional layer with stride equal to kernel size. For example, a $224\times224$ image divided into $16\times16$ patches yields $14\times14=196$ patch tokens, each represented as a vector of dimension $D=768$ in a ViT-Base model~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}.

In addition to patch tokens, a learnable classification token is commonly prepended to the sequence. This token aggregates global information through self-attention and is used as the image-level representation for classification tasks, following a design originally introduced in BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} and later adopted for vision transformers. The resulting input to the transformer encoder is therefore a sequence of $N+1$ tokens, where $N$ is the number of image patches.However, this is not a requirement and other architectures like the Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} perform some method of aggregating all the tokens into a single vector after the final layer.

The self-attention mechanism is permutation-invariant and does not encode spatial order by itself; therefore, positional information must be explicitly injected into the token embeddings. Early transformer models employed fixed sinusoidal positional encodings~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, whereas ViT introduced learned absolute positional embeddings~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} that are added to the patch and classification tokens. These embeddings allow the model to associate each token with a specific spatial location in the image grid, enabling meaningful spatial reasoning.

Absolute positional embeddings require special consideration when the input image resolution differs from the pretrained configuration. Since the learned positional embedding table is tied to a fixed number of tokens (e.g., 196 patches for $224\times224$ inputs), changing the image size alters the number and arrangement of patches, resulting in a mismatch between pretrained positional embeddings and the new token grid. To address this, common strategies include interpolating the pretrained positional embeddings to the new spatial resolution or reinitializing and retraining them. While interpolation often works well in practice, it introduces an implicit assumption of spatial smoothness and may slightly degrade performance, particularly when scaling to very small or highly distorted inputs~\cite{ranftl2021dpt,bolya2024window_attention}.

To mitigate these issues, several later architectures replace absolute positional embeddings with relative positional encodings or biases. For example, models such as the Swin Transformer~\cite{liu2021swin} incorporate relative position bias directly into the attention computation, allowing the model to encode spatial relationships between tokens independently of the absolute image size. This design improves generalization to arbitrary resolutions and simplifies deployment across varying input dimensions, which is advantageous for embedded and real-world vision systems where input sizes may vary.

\subsection{Architectural Survey of Efficient Vision Models}\label{sec:arch-survey}
The basic ViT described above is often called a “columnar” or non-hierarchical transformer: it processes tokens at one resolution throughout all layers. Furthermore, the original ViT required a significant amount of training data to achieve competitive performance. Numerous architectural variants have been proposed to improve efficiency and performance for vision tasks; a few major variations and CNN baselines relevant to embedded systems are highlighted below.

\paragraph{Data-Efficient Image Transformer (DeiT, 2021):} The DeiT model by Touvron et al.~\cite{touvron2021deit} was a crucial step in making transformers practical without massive external data. Architecturally, DeiT is essentially the same as the original ViT (a “base” 12-layer transformer with 86 million parameters) but its innovation lies in the training method. DeiT introduced a knowledge distillation strategy tailored for transformers: a learnable distillation token is appended alongside the class token, and the transformer is trained to predict a teacher CNN’s class logits via this token. By using a pre-trained ResNet as teacher and extensive data augmentation and regularization, DeiT achieved 83.1\% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet using only ImageNet-1k data (no extra pre-training)~\cite{touvron2021deit}. This matches the performance of much larger ViTs that were pre-trained on hundred-million image corpora. The ability to train on a standard dataset with a single GPU machine (in under 3 days) greatly broadened the accessibility of ViTs.

\paragraph{Swin Transformer (2021):} Swin Transformer by Liu et al.~\cite{liu2021swin} is a hierarchical ViT for classification and dense prediction. It restricts MHSA to non-overlapping local windows, typically $7\times7$ tokens. For $N$ tokens of width $D$, global attention has an attention-interaction cost of $\mathcal{O}(N^2D)$, whereas windows of $M\times M$ tokens reduce this term to $\mathcal{O}(NM^2D)$: linear in token count for fixed window size.

Two design choices preserve useful context despite this restriction. First, consecutive blocks alternate regular and shifted window partitions, connecting tokens that previously belonged to separate windows. Repeated blocks therefore expand the receptive field without computing every token pair in each layer. Second, patch merging combines each $2\times2$ neighborhood into one wider token between stages, reducing token count fourfold while retaining a learned representation of the neighborhood. Early stages capture local detail, and later stages aggregate coarser, broader context. These cross-window connections and representations at multiple scales support recognition accuracy while reducing attention cost; they do not guarantee identical accuracy to global attention for every task~\cite{liu2021swin}.

\paragraph{Pyramid Vision Transformer (PVT, 2021):} PVT by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2021pvt} was another early effort to adapt transformers as a general-purpose backbone. PVT introduces a pyramid of transformer stages to handle high-resolution inputs efficiently. In PVT, the image is initially patched into a large number of tokens (e.g. $4\times4$ patches) for the first stage, but then at each subsequent stage the tokens are merged (concatenating and projecting patches, similar to pooling) to progressively halve the resolution. This yields a pyramid of feature maps at multiple scales, much like a CNN. By the final stage, the token count is greatly reduced, addressing ViT’s high memory use on large feature maps. Notably, PVT uses full global attention within each stage (no windowing), but because later stages have few tokens, the overall cost remains manageable. The designers also modified the attention to include a spatial reduction on the attention keys/values for further speedup in high-resolution stages. PVT retains the benefit of both worlds: it has the global receptive field of transformers and the pyramidal structure of CNNs~\cite{wang2021pvt}.

\paragraph{MobileViT (2022):} MobileViT by Mehta \& Rastegari~\cite{mehta2022mobilevit} is a vision transformer specifically designed for mobile and edge devices. The motivation of MobileViT is to combine the strengths of CNNs and ViTs to create a lightweight model with both local and global feature modeling. The architecture of MobileViT is hybrid: it interleaves convolutional layers with transformer layers in a sequential block. A typical MobileViT block uses a small CNN to extract low-level features, then unfolds those features into a sequence of tokens which are processed by a transformer encoder (attention + FFN), and then folds the tokens back to feature map form which is fused with the convolutional features. This design treats transformers “as convolutions,” effectively inserting a global processing unit within a local CNN context. MobileViT also uses very compact dimensions (e.g. embedding dim 144 or 192) and only a few attention layers, making it comparable in size to mobile CNNs~\cite{mehta2022mobilevit}. MobileViT demonstrates a feasible recipe: use mostly efficient convolutions, but allocate a fraction of computation to MHSA to boost representation power.

\paragraph{ConvNeXt (2022):} ConvNeXt by Liu et al.~\cite{liu2022convnext} is a pure convolutional network, included here as a point of comparison because it was directly inspired by the design patterns of transformers. The authors of ConvNeXt asked: if we take a ResNet and modernize it with the tricks that make ViTs successful, can CNNs still compete? They progressively modified a standard ResNet: replacing $3\times3$ convolutions with larger $7\times7$ kernels (to increase receptive field, analogous to global attention), using only depthwise separable convolutions (like a transformer’s factorized attention), moving the location of normalization layers (using layer normalization instead of batch normalization), and increasing network width and depth based on transformer scaling trends. The outcome: ConvNeXt proved to be on par with transformers~\cite{liu2022convnext}. The significance for embedded systems is that CNNs are not obsolete – by adopting transformer-era improvements, CNNs can attain similar accuracy and often with lower inference overhead on certain hardware. Many edge deployments (e.g. DSP or NPU accelerators) are still more optimized for convolutions operations than for MHSA~\cite{Zhao2025H4HNAS,LiPaolieriGolubchik2025,Quadric2023ViT}. ConvNeXt offers a compelling alternative when one needs the best of both worlds: it yields transformer-level accuracy but uses standard convolutions layers that may run more efficiently on existing embedded inference engines. In practice, the choice between ConvNeXt and a ViT may come down to the specific hardware and compiler support. ConvNeXt is one indication of how the gap between CNNs and Transformers has narrowed, with both paradigms borrowing ideas from each other.

\paragraph{ResNet (2016):} Finally, we consider the classical ResNet architecture by He et al.~\cite{he2016resnet} as a baseline. ResNets introduced the concept of residual learning through skip connections, enabling very deep CNNs to train effectively. A ResNet’s computational structure is a chain of convolutional blocks with occasional downsampling, and its strong hierarchical feature extraction made it the dominant vision backbone of the late 2010s~\cite{he2016resnet}. ResNets are relevant to embedded vision as they are highly optimized in many libraries and hardware accelerators; a ResNet-50 can often run in real-time on mobile NPUs after quantization~\cite{ignatov2021ai_benchmark,android2025optimization}. However, compared to newer architectures, ResNets have relatively lower accuracy at a given model size and lack the global attention mechanism of ViTs. Throughout this chapter, we use ResNet variants as a conventional baseline to quantify the improvements made by transformer-based models. Some efficient ViTs now exceed ResNet-50's accuracy while being smaller or faster, which is one indication of how much the field has progressed.

\section{Optimization Strategies for Embedded Training}\label{sec:optim}
Deploying vision transformers on embedded platforms requires more than efficient architectural design---understood as the static layer-wise parameterization and data flow of the model---but also requires training and adaptation strategies that account for constraints on memory, computation, and energy, as well as risks of overfitting. While most embedded devices are ill-suited for large-scale model optimization, careful separation of offline training and lightweight adaptation enables transformer-based models to be deployed effectively in practice. This section outlines common training pipelines for embedded vision transformers, discusses the trade-offs between offline training and on-device adaptation, and introduces optimization techniques like parameter-efficient fine-tuning, knowledge distillation, quantization, and pruning that facilitate deployment under resource constraints. These strategies form the foundation for the TinyFace case study presented in this chapter.

\subsection{Training Pipeline and Resource Constraints}
In most real-world scenarios, training large vision transformer models is performed offline on high-performance servers or cloud-based GPUs rather than directly on embedded devices~\cite{mlsys2023ondevice_chapter,ambient2025training_vs_inference}. Vision transformers typically require substantial computational resources, including high memory bandwidth and large batch sizes, to train effectively. Embedded platforms, by contrast, are optimized for inference workloads and generally lack the memory capacity, floating-point throughput, and energy budget required for full model training.

As a result, the dominant training pipeline follows a two-stage approach: models are pretrained or fine-tuned offline using hardware with significantly few resource limitations and then deployed on embedded devices exclusively for inference. This paradigm allows developers to exploit advanced optimizers, extensive data augmentation, and long training schedules without imposing additional burdens on the target device. Early embedded machine learning systems largely adhered to this model, focusing on low-latency, on-device inference with pretrained networks~\cite{mlsys2023ondevice_chapter}.

Although on-device training has gained interest due to its potential benefits for privacy, personalization, and domain adaptation, it remains challenging for transformer-based models. Training workloads are significantly more demanding than inference, and even short fine-tuning runs can exhaust memory, increase thermal load, or reduce device lifespan. Consequently, for embedded vision transformers, complete training from scratch is almost always performed offline, with the device serving as a deployment target rather than a training platform. This separation places greater importance on the robustness and adaptability of the trained model to real-world operating conditions~\cite{zhu2024ondevice_training_survey,mlsys2023ondevice_chapter}.

\subsection{Offline Training vs.\ On-Device Adaptation}
While offline training is the preferred approach for developing and optimizing vision transformers, many embedded applications require models to adapt over time to new data, users, or environments. In such cases, a hybrid strategy is often employed in which computationally intensive training is performed offline, while limited adaptation is handled either on-device or through periodic updates from a server.

Offline training offers several advantages, including the ability to use large batch sizes, sophisticated optimization algorithms, and diverse data augmentation strategies. Once trained, models can be compressed through techniques such as quantization or pruning and deployed to embedded devices for efficient inference. When updates are needed, retraining or fine-tuning can be performed offline and redistributed to devices, ensuring consistent performance without incurring on-device training costs.

On-device adaptation, when used, is typically restricted to lightweight updates such as incremental learning of new classes, personalization to user-specific data, or calibration to changing sensor conditions~\cite{nadalini2025odl_tiny,mlsys2023ondevice_chapter}. For example, an embedded camera system may adapt a face recognition model to new identities observed in the field. However, these benefits must be weighed against practical constraints: training even a small subset of parameters can significantly increase energy consumption, reduce responsiveness, and stress limited hardware resources. Furthermore, some optimization techniques and adaptive optimizers are poorly supported on embedded NPUs or DSPs~\cite{mlsys2023ondevice_chapter}.

To balance these trade-offs, techniques such as parameter-efficient fine-tuning, federated learning, and split learning have been explored~\cite{houlsby2019adapter,mlsys2023ondevice_chapter}. These methods aim to minimize on-device computation while preserving adaptability, often by limiting the number of trainable parameters or offloading portions of training to external servers. In practice, offline training remains the primary mechanism for developing embedded vision transformers, while on-device adaptation is applied selectively in scenarios where privacy, personalization, or environmental variability justify the additional resource cost.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Tuning (LoRA)}
When adapting large vision transformers to a new task or dataset, updating all model parameters is often unnecessary and computationally expensive. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PET) methods address this issue by restricting training to a small subset of parameters while keeping the pretrained backbone fixed. Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a prominent and effective PET technique that has been successfully applied to vision transformers.

LoRA operates by freezing the original model weights and injecting additional trainable low-rank matrices into selected layers, most commonly the linear projections within multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and, in some cases, the feed-forward networks (FFNs). Rather than updating a full weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, LoRA introduces an additive update
\[
W' = W + \Delta W, \quad \Delta W = A B^\top,
\]
where $W' \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the adapted weight matrix and $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ and $B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$ are trainable matrices of rank $r \ll d$. The original weight $W$ remains frozen, and only the low-rank factors $A$ and $B$ are optimized. Typical rank values such as $r=4$ or $r=8$ introduce only a small fraction of additional parameters while allowing sufficient expressive capacity for task adaptation.

This formulation significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters and associated optimizer state, often to well under $1\%$ of the full model size. Hu et al.~\cite{hu2022lora} demonstrated that LoRA can achieve performance comparable to full fine-tuning across a range of tasks while dramatically lowering training cost. From an optimization standpoint, LoRA also improves stability by constraining updates to a low-dimensional subspace, reducing the risk of overfitting or catastrophic forgetting, as demonstrated in works like PETALface~\cite{narayan2025petalface}.

For embedded and resource-constrained settings, the advantages of LoRA are particularly compelling. Fine-tuning can be performed with smaller batch sizes, reduced memory consumption, and faster convergence, making it feasible to adapt large pretrained models using modest hardware. However, it should be noted that LoRA does not reduce the memory footprint training because all hidden states are required to train the new low-rank matrices~\cite{Wang2025HyCLoRA}. Because the backbone weights remain unchanged, LoRA preserves the model’s original representational capabilities while learning task-specific corrections. In low-resolution face recognition scenarios, for example, LoRA enables a transformer pretrained on high-quality facial imagery to adapt to datasets such as TinyFace without degrading its performance on higher-resolution domains~\cite{narayan2025petalface}. Recent works such as PETALface and FaceMoE~\cite{narayan2025petalface,narayan2024facemoe} further demonstrate that conditioning or gating LoRA updates can improve robustness across varying image qualities, though the underlying low-rank adaptation principle remains broadly applicable.

From a deployed embedded systems perspective, frozen-backbone LoRA can also reduce the payload required to distribute model updates. Devices that share the same unchanged backbone need only receive the adapter factors, whose size depends on their rank and the targeted projections. These optimizer-state and update-payload savings apply to standard LoRA with a frozen backbone. The preliminary sweeps in Section~\ref{sec:training-protocol} used this frozen-backbone procedure, whereas the final test-set comparisons used LoRA with progressive backbone unfreezing. In the latter procedure the unfrozen pretrained weights also change, so their updates must be distributed along with the adapters. The final hybrid results therefore do not establish the training-memory or adapter-only transmission savings of standard LoRA.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation}
Knowledge distillation (KD) is a widely used technique for transferring knowledge from a large, high-capacity \emph{teacher} model to a smaller or more efficient \emph{student} model. Rather than training the student solely on hard class labels, distillation encourages the student to match the teacher’s output distribution, intermediate representations, or both. This process provides additional supervisory signals that can improve convergence, generalization, and sample efficiency, particularly when training compact models.

In the context of vision transformers, knowledge distillation has played a critical role in reducing data and compute requirements. The Data-efficient Image Transformer (DeiT) demonstrated that ViTs could be trained effectively on ImageNet-1k without large-scale pretraining by distilling knowledge from a convolutional neural network teacher. DeiT introduced a learnable \emph{distillation token} alongside the standard classification token, allowing the transformer to explicitly model the teacher’s predictions through an auxiliary supervision path. This design enabled ViTs to achieve competitive accuracy with CNNs while significantly reducing the reliance on massive external datasets~\cite{touvron2021deit}.

Beyond output-level distillation, several works have explored feature-level and attention-level distillation for transformers. In these approaches, the student is encouraged to align intermediate feature maps, attention matrices, or token embeddings with those of the teacher. Such strategies are particularly effective when distilling from a larger transformer into a smaller one, as they transfer structural inductive biases and attention patterns that are not captured by class probabilities alone. For embedded deployment, this enables smaller transformer models to inherit much of the representational power of larger backbones while maintaining lower latency and memory footprints~\cite{zhang2022minivit,wu2022tinyvit}.

From an embedded systems perspective, knowledge distillation is especially attractive because it shifts computational burden entirely to the training phase. The teacher model is used only offline, and the distilled student incurs no additional inference cost. This makes KD complementary to architectural efficiency and parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA. In practice, distillation is often combined with model compression techniques, including quantization or pruning, to further reduce inference cost without significant accuracy degradation.

\subsection{Quantization and Pruning}
In addition to architectural design and training-time optimizations, post-training model compression techniques such as quantization and pruning are essential for deploying vision transformers on embedded platforms. These methods aim to reduce memory footprint, computational cost, and energy consumption while preserving as much predictive performance as possible.

\paragraph{Quantization:}
Quantization reduces the numerical precision of model parameters and activations, typically from 32-bit floating point (FP32) to lower-bit representations such as 16-bit floating point (FP16) or 8-bit integer (INT8). On embedded hardware, lower-precision arithmetic is often significantly faster and more energy-efficient, and it allows models to fit within limited on-chip memory~\cite{Roth2024ResourceEff}. For vision transformers, quantization can be applied to linear projections in attention layers, feed-forward networks, and embedding layers~\cite{Li2023IViT}. Post-training quantization (PTQ) is attractive for deployment because it does not require retraining the model; instead, calibration data is used to estimate appropriate scaling factors~\cite{Shang2024PTQ}. However, transformers can be more sensitive to quantization error than CNNs, particularly in the attention softmax and layer normalization operations~\cite{Ranjan2024MixQViT}. Quantization-aware training (QAT) addresses this issue by simulating quantization effects during training, allowing the model to adapt its parameters to reduced precision~\cite{IBM2023QAT}. In practice, many embedded deployments adopt mixed-precision schemes, retaining higher precision for numerically sensitive operations (e.g., softmax and normalization) while quantizing the bulk of linear layers to INT8. Such hybrid approaches often achieve substantial speedups with minimal accuracy loss~\cite{Lin2022FQViT}.

\paragraph{Pruning:}
Pruning reduces model complexity by removing parameters or structures that contribute little to overall performance. In unstructured pruning, individual weights with small magnitude are set to zero, yielding sparse matrices. While this can significantly reduce parameter count, unstructured sparsity is difficult to exploit on general-purpose hardware without specialized sparse kernels. As a result, structured pruning is often preferred for embedded systems~\cite{Jiang2023SingleShot}.

Structured pruning removes entire components such as attention heads, channels, or even transformer layers~\cite{Roth2024ResourceEff}. For vision transformers, common strategies include pruning redundant attention heads, reducing the width of feed-forward networks, or dropping entire encoder blocks in deeper layers. Because the transformer encoder is modular and highly repetitive, it lends itself well to such coarse-grained pruning~\cite{Park2023PTPrune}. Structured pruning produces dense sub-networks that map efficiently onto existing hardware accelerators~\cite{Roth2024ResourceEff}.

\paragraph{Embedded Deployment Considerations:}
From an embedded systems perspective, the effectiveness of quantization and pruning depends strongly on hardware support and software tooling~\cite{Ngo2025EdgeInference}. Many NPUs and DSPs provide native INT8 or mixed-precision acceleration but offer limited support for sparsity~\cite{Roth2024ResourceEff}. Consequently, structured pruning combined with quantization-aware training is often the most practical compression strategy for real-world deployment~\cite{liu2025modelcompression,Jiang2023SingleShot}. When carefully applied, these techniques enable vision transformers to meet strict latency, memory, and power constraints while retaining much of their original representational strength~\cite{Li2023IViT}.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:experimental}
This section describes the dataset, preprocessing choices, training protocol, and evaluation methodology used to assess the performance and deployability of vision transformer models in an embedded setting. The experimental design emphasizes both biometric accuracy and computational efficiency, reflecting the dual objectives of recognition performance and practical deployment.

\subsection{Dataset Selection: TinyFace}
The TinyFace dataset was selected as the primary benchmark for this study due to its emphasis on extremely low-resolution facial imagery. TinyFace contains over 160,000 face images spanning 5,139 identities, with an average native resolution of approximately $30\times30$ pixels. Images exhibit significant degradation due to limited spatial resolution, compression artifacts, pose variation, and illumination changes. These characteristics make TinyFace a particularly challenging dataset for face recognition and a strong proxy for embedded vision scenarios such as long-range surveillance, low-bandwidth sensing, and resource-constrained edge devices~\cite{cheng2018lowresface}.

Unlike many standard face recognition benchmarks that assume high-quality facial crops, TinyFace stresses a model’s ability to extract discriminative features from minimal visual information. This aligns well with the goals of this work, which seeks to evaluate whether scalable vision transformers can remain effective when both input resolution and deployment hardware are severely constrained.

\subsection{Image Resolution and Preprocessing}
All TinyFace images were resized to a fixed spatial resolution of $96\times96$ pixels prior to being passed to the models. This resolution was chosen as a compromise between preserving spatial detail and avoiding excessive upsampling of the original low-resolution images. Given an average native resolution of approximately $30\times30$ pixels, more aggressive resizing (e.g., $112\times112$ or $224\times224$) was found to introduce substantial interpolation artifacts without meaningful gains in discriminative information.

From a transformer perspective, a $96\times96$ input resolution provides a sufficient number of patch tokens to enable effective self-attention while keeping computational cost manageable. For example, with a patch size of $16\times16$, this resolution yields $6\times6=36$ tokens, striking a balance between spatial expressiveness and efficiency. This choice also aligns with embedded deployment goals, where minimizing token count directly reduces attention complexity and memory usage.

Training augmentations included random horizontal flips, random rotation ($\pm 15^{\circ}$), color jitter (brightness, contrast, saturation, and hue), and random affine translations ($\pm 10\%$). All images were normalized using ImageNet statistics (mean $=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406]$, std $=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]$)~\cite{russakovsky2015imagenet}. Evaluation used only resizing and normalization without augmentations.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:training-protocol}
Prior to final training runs, Bayesian hyperparameter sweeps were conducted to identify stable and effective configurations for each model family. These sweeps explored learning rate, weight decay, head margin, and LoRA-specific parameters (rank, alpha, dropout), with the goal of identifying hyperparameter regions that produced consistent convergence.

\paragraph{Standard Frozen-Backbone LoRA Sweeps.}
The preliminary AdaFace sweeps trained only the LoRA adapters and classification head, leaving all pretrained backbone weights frozen for the entire run. These runs used 40 epochs, batch size 8, and adapters in the fused QKV projection only, without gradual unfreezing or backbone-bias training. The selected Swin-Base configuration used $(r,\alpha)=(24,120)$ and achieved rank-1 = 0.5814; DeiT3-Base used $(r,\alpha)=(4,24)$ and achieved 0.5891. Both values are from the \emph{EVAL} split used for hyperparameter selection and do not provide a matched test-set comparison with the later hybrid runs.

\paragraph{Baseline Training without Adapters.}
The architecture survey and the separate six-model comparison against LoRA used AdamW for 50 epochs, an 8-epoch warmup followed by cosine decay, mixed-precision training, and progressive unfreezing at five-epoch intervals. DeiT-3 unfroze its final eight blocks; Swin, ConvNeXt, PVT-v2, and ResNet unfroze their final two stages; MobileViT-v2 unfroze its final three stages. The backbone was initially frozen and only the classification head was trainable. The final Swin and DeiT-3 baseline runs used batch size 8 and AdaFace, as did the non-ResNet architecture-survey runs; the ResNet survey runs used batch size 16 and ArcFace. Margin, scale, and optimizer settings were tuned per family. The training loss is cross-entropy between margin-scaled logits and identity labels. The architecture survey and paired LoRA comparison used distinct run sets, so their baseline accuracy for a given model need not be identical.

\paragraph{LoRA with Progressive Unfreezing.}
Low-rank adapters were injected into the fused query, key, and value projection and the output projection of every MHSA block. Following Hu et al.~\cite{hu2022lora}, an adapted linear layer computes
\[
W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} \cdot BA,
\]
where $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r}$, and $\alpha$ scales the rank-$r$ update. The final hybrid runs selected for test evaluation used $(r,\alpha)=(24,120)$ for Swin-T/S, $(8,32)$ for Swin-B, and $(4,24)$ for all DeiT-3 sizes; adapter dropout was 0.05 for Swin and 0.10 for DeiT-3.

Training used AdamW for 50 epochs, batch size 8, an 8-epoch warmup followed by cosine decay, and a learning-rate floor of $0.1\%$ of the peak. Epoch indices below are zero-based. During epochs 0--4, all pretrained backbone weights were frozen; only adapters and the AdaFace classification head were trainable. For Swin, the deepest stage (stage 4) was unfrozen at epoch 5 and the preceding stage (stage 3) at epoch 10; stages 1--2, the patch embedding, and the final normalization remained frozen. For DeiT-3, the final eight of twelve blocks were unfrozen one at a time, deepest first, at epochs 5, 10, $\ldots$, 40. The first four blocks, patch/positional embeddings, classification token, and final normalization remained frozen. Unfreezing enabled every parameter in the selected module, including the original attention projections, FFN, and normalization weights; adapters remained trainable throughout.

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation Setup}\label{sec:kd-setup}
To evaluate the impact of knowledge distillation on student model performance, a feature-level distillation framework was implemented. The combined training loss is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kd-loss}
    \mathcal{L} = \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{logits}, y) + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$ is the standard cross-entropy loss with the AdaFace head, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}}$ is the cosine embedding loss between L2-normalized teacher and student feature vectors, and $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ controls the relative weighting. A trainable linear projection layer maps the student embedding dimension to the teacher's 512-dimensional space when dimensions differ.

Two teacher models, both pretrained on the large-scale WebFace4M dataset, were evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CVLFace}: A ViT-Base model with AdaFace head (114.9M parameters), accepting $112\times112$ inputs and producing 512-dimensional L2-normalized embeddings. This represents a cross-architecture teacher (ViT $\rightarrow$ Swin/DeiT student).
    \item \textbf{PETALface}: A custom 3-stage Swin Transformer with ArcFace head (213.8M parameters), accepting $120\times120$ inputs and producing 512-dimensional L2-normalized embeddings~\cite{narayan2025petalface}. This represents a same-architecture teacher for Swin students.
\end{itemize}
Both teachers are substantially larger than the student models evaluated in this study (17.4M--87.6M parameters), and their high accuracy stems in part from pretraining on WebFace4M (4.2 million identities), which is orders of magnitude larger than TinyFace. These factors---large model size, high input resolution ($112\times112$ or $120\times120$ vs.\ the student's $96\times96$), and extensive pretraining data---make the teachers impractical for direct deployment on embedded platforms. Knowledge distillation therefore serves as the mechanism to transfer the teachers' representational capacity to smaller, deployable student architectures.

Each teacher was evaluated in two modes: \emph{frozen} (original pretrained weights) and \emph{fine-tuned} (adapted to TinyFace before distillation). Student inputs are $96\times96$ images with ImageNet normalization. During KD training, they are un-normalized, resized to the teacher's input resolution, and re-normalized to $[-1, 1]$.

The default distillation weight was $\alpha = 0.7$. The ablation study tested eight values: $0.0$, $0.1$, $0.3$, $0.5$, $0.7$, $0.8$, $0.9$, and $1.0$.

KD student models were trained for 50 epochs with batch size 8, an 8-epoch warmup followed by cosine decay, and AdamW. Both \emph{Direct} (without adapters) and \emph{LoRA} (with adapters) modes used the same progressive unfreezing schedule: the final two Swin stages or final eight DeiT-3 blocks, as described in Section~\ref{sec:training-protocol}. In both modes the classification head and student-to-teacher projection were trainable from the start; LoRA adapters, when present, were also trainable throughout. Direct KD follows the no-adapter progressive-unfreezing protocol with the addition of teacher supervision and the feature projection. KD adapters used $(r,\alpha)=(24,120)$ for all Swin sizes and $(4,24)$ for DeiT-3.

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
All models were evaluated using TinyFace's matching gallery and probes, with distractor gallery images excluded. These results therefore describe a reduced-gallery protocol. Probe images are matched against enrolled identities using cosine similarity of the extracted embeddings. The primary evaluation metrics include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rank-$k$ identification rate}: The fraction of probe images for which the correct identity appears within the top $k$ gallery matches, reported for $k = 1$ through $10$.
    \item \textbf{Mean average precision (mAP)}: The mean of per-query average precision scores across all probe images.
    \item \textbf{Area under the curve (AUC)}: The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve.
\end{itemize}
Rank-1 identification rate is used as the primary metric for model selection and comparison, as it directly reflects the practical accuracy of a single-shot identification system. All experiments used a fixed random seed of 73 for reproducibility.

\subsection{ONNX Export and Quantization}\label{sec:onnx-quant-setup}
To evaluate deployment-readiness, all trained models were exported to the ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) format and quantized to reduced-precision representations. ONNX provides a hardware-agnostic inference format supported by embedded runtime engines including ONNX Runtime, TensorRT, and vendor-specific NPU compilers.

Each model was exported from its PyTorch Lightning checkpoint with \texttt{torch.onnx.export} at opset version 18 with dynamic batch axes, producing FP32 ONNX graphs. For LoRA-adapted models, low-rank matrices were algebraically merged into the base weights prior to export (i.e., $W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA$), yielding a standard dense model with no adapter overhead at inference. For KD-trained models, only the student backbone was exported; the teacher and projection layers were discarded.

Two quantization schemes were applied:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FP16}: Static weight conversion from FP32 to IEEE 754 half-precision via onnxconverter-common, reducing model size by approximately $2\times$.
    \item \textbf{INT8}: Dynamic quantization of linear layer weights (\texttt{MatMul} and \texttt{Gemm} operators) to 8-bit integers via ONNX Runtime's \texttt{quantize\_dynamic}, targeting approximately $4\times$ size reduction.
\end{itemize}
All FP32 ONNX exports were validated against the original PyTorch models by computing cosine similarity between output embeddings on a shared calibration set, requiring $>0.999$ similarity. Each quantized model was then independently evaluated on the TinyFace test set using ONNX Runtime inference (CPU) to measure the actual impact of reduced precision on recognition accuracy.

All five deployment configurations were also evaluated directly in TensorRT on the matching gallery and probes, without distractors. The head-pruned DeiT3-S INT8 engine used entropy calibration on 512 training images, excluding validation and test identities; its learned linear projections ran in INT8 while other layers retained floating point.

Runtime resources were measured in three 45-second sustained runs per engine after warm-up, with inputs resident on the GPU. On the Jetson's shared-memory system, peak additional memory was estimated from the reduction in available RAM relative to an idle baseline; reported ranges span the three observed peaks. Energy per inference was obtained by integrating total module input power (\texttt{VDD\_IN}), including idle draw, over the completed inference window~\cite{NVIDIAJetsonPower}.

\subsection{Structured Pruning}\label{sec:pruning-setup}
Structured pruning was evaluated on Swin-T, Swin-B, DeiT3-S, and DeiT3-B. Fifteen configurations were derived from five checkpoints: Swin-T Baseline, Swin-T KD Best ($\alpha{=}0.0$), DeiT3-S Baseline, Swin-B Baseline, and DeiT3-B KD Best ($\alpha{=}0.1$, fine-tuned CVLFace teacher). Nine earlier configurations also covered the DeiT3-B baseline and Swin-B with LoRA and progressive unfreezing, giving 24 pruned models in total. Two pruning strategies were evaluated:

\paragraph{Attention Head Pruning.}
For each multi-head self-attention (MHSA) layer, a per-head importance score is computed from the entrywise L1 norms of the query, key, value, and output projection weight slices associated with that head:
\[
    I_h = \|W^Q_h\|_1 + \|W^K_h\|_1 + \|W^V_h\|_1 + \|W^{\text{proj}}_h\|_1,
\]
where $W^Q_h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d}$ denotes the projection weights for head $h$ of dimension $d_k = d/N_H$. Heads with the lowest importance scores are removed, and the corresponding weight slices are physically deleted from the QKV and output projection matrices. This produces a structurally smaller model with fewer attention heads per layer. Pruning ratios of 10\%, 25\%, and 50\% of heads were evaluated.

\paragraph{Block Dropping.}
Entire transformer encoder blocks are removed using a last-first ordering: the deepest eligible blocks are removed first~\cite{Park2023PTPrune}. For DeiT3-S and DeiT3-B (12 blocks each), 2 and 3 blocks were removed, respectively. For Swin, removal was restricted to its largest stage (stage 3): 2 of its 6 blocks for Swin-T, and 5 of its 18 blocks for Swin-B. The complete Swin-T and Swin-B backbones contain 12 and 24 blocks, respectively.

All pruned models were fine-tuned for 10 epochs with all parameters unfrozen, using AdamW (lr = $3 \times 10^{-5}$, weight decay = 0.05) with cosine annealing, to recover accuracy lost during pruning. Pruned models were then exported to ONNX and quantized to FP16 and INT8 using the same pipeline described in Section~\ref{sec:onnx-quant-setup}, yielding 72 pruned+quantized model variants (24 pruned configurations $\times$ 3 precisions).

\section{Performance Analysis}\label{sec:performance}
This section presents the experimental results across six lines of evaluation: (1) baseline architecture comparison, (2) LoRA with progressive backbone unfreezing, (3) knowledge distillation with multiple teacher configurations, (4) an ablation study on the distillation loss mixing coefficient, (5) ONNX export and quantization of selected models, and (6) structured pruning combined with quantization. Results are analyzed through the lens of both recognition accuracy and model efficiency to inform deployment decisions for embedded systems.

\subsection{Baseline Architecture Comparison}
Sixteen model variants spanning six architecture families were trained on TinyFace using the baseline protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:training-protocol}. Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-comparison} presents the rank-1 identification rates.

\begin{figure}[!hbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{baseline_comparison.pdf}
    \caption{Rank-1 identification rate on the TinyFace test set for all 16 baseline model variants, sorted by performance. On this low-resolution dataset, vision transformers (DeiT-3, Swin) outperform CNNs (ResNet, ConvNeXt) and hybrid architectures.}
    \label{fig:baseline-comparison}
\end{figure}

Several observations follow from the baseline comparison. First, vision transformers outperform CNNs on this low-resolution task. DeiT3-Base achieves the highest rank-1 rate of 0.583, followed by DeiT3-Medium (0.570) and Swin-Tiny (0.562). All three ResNet variants rank at the bottom; ResNet-200 (0.468) performs worst despite having 62.6M parameters. One plausible explanation is that global self-attention provides an advantage when discriminative information must be extracted from severely limited spatial resolution, as is the case with TinyFace's $30\times30$ average native input.

Second, larger models within a family do not always perform better. Swin-Tiny (27.5M parameters, rank-1 = 0.562) outperforms Swin-Base (86.7M, 0.550), and DeiT3-Medium (38.3M, 0.570) is roughly on par with DeiT3-Base (85.8M, 0.583) at less than half the parameter count. For ResNets, scaling from 50 to 200 layers actually \emph{decreases} performance (0.476 $\rightarrow$ 0.468), indicating that additional capacity does not compensate for the architectural limitations of purely local feature extraction at these resolutions. This inverse scaling matters for embedded deployment: smaller transformer variants achieve better accuracy and lower computational cost at the same time.

Figure~\ref{fig:params-vs-accuracy} shows the relationship between model size and accuracy.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{params_vs_accuracy.pdf}
    \caption{Model parameter count versus rank-1 identification rate. Student models (circles) range from 17.4M to 87.6M parameters, while teacher models (stars) used for knowledge distillation are substantially larger (CVLFace 114.9M, PETALface 213.8M). The teachers' size and input resolution requirements make them impractical for embedded deployment, motivating the distillation approach studied in Section~\ref{sec:kd-setup}.}
    \label{fig:params-vs-accuracy}
\end{figure}

Third, ConvNeXt models, despite incorporating many transformer-inspired design principles, perform only slightly above their ResNet counterparts and substantially below DeiT-3 and Swin. ConvNeXt-Base (87.6M) achieves 0.543, lower than Swin-Tiny at one-third the parameters. At the extremely low input resolutions characteristic of TinyFace, explicit self-attention---not merely large receptive fields or modern training practices---appears to be the dominant factor.

Figure~\ref{fig:params-vs-accuracy} also plots the teacher models used in subsequent knowledge distillation experiments (Section~\ref{sec:kd-setup}). The CVLFace ViT-Base teacher (114.9M parameters) achieves rank-1 rates of 0.716 (frozen) and 0.781 (fine-tuned), while the PETALface Swin teacher (213.8M parameters) achieves 0.424 (frozen) and 0.663 (fine-tuned). Although the teachers offer substantially higher accuracy---particularly after fine-tuning on TinyFace---their parameter counts of 114.9M and 213.8M exceed the largest student models by 30--150\%, placing them well outside the feasible range for embedded deployment. This performance--size gap motivates the use of knowledge distillation to transfer teacher-level representational capacity to compact student architectures.

\subsection{LoRA with Progressive Backbone Unfreezing}
Following the baseline comparison, the top-performing architecture families (Swin and DeiT-3) were fine-tuned using LoRA with progressive backbone unfreezing as described in Section~\ref{sec:training-protocol}. Test-set results compare the final hybrid runs with their separate no-adapter baselines. The standard frozen-backbone LoRA sweep results reported in Section~\ref{sec:training-protocol} use the EVAL split and are not plotted in this figure.

The results show an architecture-dependent response to LoRA with progressive unfreezing. For Swin models, the hybrid protocol consistently improves accuracy over the baseline: Swin-Tiny improves from 0.534 to 0.562 ($+$0.028 rank-1), Swin-Small from 0.539 to 0.554 ($+$0.015), and Swin-Base from 0.526 to 0.536 ($+$0.010). DeiT-3 models exhibit the opposite trend: the hybrid protocol degrades performance for DeiT3-Small (0.552 $\rightarrow$ 0.527, $-$0.025), DeiT3-Medium (0.570 $\rightarrow$ 0.533, $-$0.037), and DeiT3-Base (0.583 $\rightarrow$ 0.550, $-$0.033). Figure~\ref{fig:baseline-vs-lora} summarizes this comparison.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{baseline_vs_lora.pdf}
    \caption{Test-set comparison of progressive backbone unfreezing without adapters (Baseline) and with LoRA adapters (LoRA) across Swin and DeiT-3 variants. The hybrid improves Swin models but degrades DeiT-3 performance under the separately tuned configurations. Standard frozen-backbone LoRA sweep metrics are not included.}
    \label{fig:baseline-vs-lora}
\end{figure}

Architectural differences may contribute to this divergence, but the experiment does not isolate their effect. Swin's hierarchical local-window attention and DeiT-3's global MHSA operate with different unfreezing schedules and final trainable fractions (Table~\ref{tab:lora-trainability}). Each paired baseline and hybrid shared batch size, training duration, and unfreezing schedule, but used separately tuned optimizer, dropout, and head settings. Because unfrozen weights receive unrestricted updates, these results do not demonstrate that a low-rank constraint alone improves Swin or limits DeiT-3. The earlier frozen-backbone sweeps used a different split, duration, and adapter targeting, so they do not supply a matched test-set control for that explanation.

The CMC curves show that the DeiT3-Base baseline leads at every plotted rank: its rank-1/rank-10 rates are 0.583/0.741, compared with 0.550/0.710 for DeiT3-Base with LoRA and unfreezing. Swin-Tiny improves from 0.534/0.707 to 0.562/0.727 under the hybrid protocol. All four transformer configurations outperform the plotted ResNet-50 baseline across ranks 1--10, showing that their advantage extends beyond rank-1 identification in this evaluation (Figure~\ref{fig:cmc-curves}).

\end{figure}

\subsection{Knowledge Distillation Results}

Knowledge distillation experiments were conducted for all Swin and DeiT-3 student models using both CVLFace and PETALface teachers in frozen and fine-tuned modes.

Several findings emerge from the distillation study. First, KD with the default $\alpha = 0.7$ provides only marginal improvements for Swin students ($+$0.000 to $+$0.005 rank-1) and slight degradation for DeiT-3 students ($-$0.003 to $-$0.011). The higher-accuracy teacher (CVLFace ViT-Base, rank-1 = 0.716 frozen, 0.781 fine-tuned) does not translate its substantial performance advantage into student accuracy at this default mixing ratio. On this dataset the bottleneck appears to lie in the student's capacity to extract discriminative features from a $96\times96$ input rather than in the quality of the supervisory signal. Table~\ref{tab:kd-results} summarizes the key results.

Second, teacher fine-tuning does not reliably improve student performance. Fine-tuning dramatically improves the teacher's own accuracy (CVLFace: $0.716 \rightarrow 0.781$; PETALface: $0.424 \rightarrow 0.663$), but the resulting KD students perform inconsistently, sometimes better and sometimes worse than those trained with frozen teachers. This challenges the common assumption that a stronger teacher necessarily produces a stronger student.

Third, the teacher architecture matters but not in the expected direction. Despite PETALface achieving substantially lower accuracy on TinyFace (rank-1 = 0.424 frozen vs.\ CVLFace's 0.716), it occasionally produces competitive students, particularly for Swin-Tiny with LoRA (rank-1 = 0.567). One explanation is a same-architecture inductive-bias benefit (Swin teacher $\rightarrow$ Swin student) over cross-architecture transfer (ViT teacher $\rightarrow$ Swin student); another is that the PETALface teacher's lower TinyFace accuracy does not fully reflect the usefulness of its intermediate feature representations for guiding student training.

Finally, several Swin-Small and Swin-Base runs combining LoRA, progressive unfreezing, and KD degraded to rank-1 values of 0.35--0.41 across both teacher types. This instability was not universal: Swin-Small with the fine-tuned CVLFace teacher achieved 0.559 (Table~\ref{tab:kd-results}). The results identify a sensitivity of the combined training protocol; they do not isolate LoRA, unfreezing, or KD as its cause.

\subsection{Distillation Loss Mixing Ablation}

The default mixing coefficient $\alpha = 0.7$ was selected based on common practice~\cite{hinton2015distillation}; however, the modest KD gains in the previous section motivated a systematic ablation. Four representative configurations were swept across $\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0\}$, where $\alpha = 0.0$ corresponds to pure KD loss and $\alpha = 1.0$ to pure cross-entropy (no distillation). Figure~\ref{fig:alpha-ablation} presents the results, and Table~\ref{tab:alpha-optimal} summarizes the optimal configurations.

\end{figure}

The ablation reveals several findings. The default $\alpha = 0.7$ is suboptimal for all four configurations. Optima fall at $\alpha = 0.0$ (Config A) and $\alpha = 0.1$ (Configs B, C, D), indicating that heavily weighting the feature-level KD loss over the hard classification loss is beneficial. The effect is most pronounced for Config A (Swin-Tiny + CVLFace frozen), where pure KD ($\alpha = 0.0$) achieves rank-1 = 0.616---an improvement of $+$0.067 over $\alpha = 0.7$ and $+$0.054 over the best non-KD result (Swin-Tiny LoRA, 0.562). This is the strongest student result in the study.

The behavior at $\alpha = 0.0$ (pure KD) depends on the teacher. With CVLFace---which achieves higher accuracy on TinyFace---pure KD achieves the best result (0.616); with PETALface---which is less well-adapted to this dataset in the frozen setting---pure KD collapses to 0.416--0.450, performing worse than no KD at all. On this task, then, a teacher with high task-specific accuracy appears necessary for pure feature-level distillation to succeed. Whether this finding generalizes to other datasets and teacher--student pairings is an open question.

For the mid-range $\alpha$ values (0.1--0.9), performance is remarkably stable, with spreads of only 0.013--0.022 rank-1 across all non-extreme values. This robustness suggests that once a small amount of classification loss is present to anchor training, the exact mixing ratio matters less than whether distillation is included at all.

Several findings emerge from the comprehensive pruning experiments across all four architectures:

\paragraph{The preferred pruning axis depends on the architecture.}
Block dropping improves the Swin-T and Swin-B baselines by $+$0.010 and $+$0.028 rank-1 at INT8, respectively, while head pruning degrades both. DeiT3-S instead benefits from 10\% head pruning ($+$0.013) and loses accuracy under block dropping. DeiT3-B KD similarly retains more accuracy with 10\% head pruning (0.591) than with three blocks removed (0.548). These tested removal levels are not matched for parameter reduction; they support an architecture-dependent choice rather than a universal ranking of pruning methods.

\paragraph{DeiT3 is more robust to head pruning than Swin.}
DeiT3-S with 10\% head pruning improves rank-1 from 0.552 to 0.565 ($+$0.013), and DeiT3-B KD retains 0.591 out of 0.593. At INT8, Swin-T head pruning degrades from its FP32 source rate of 0.534 to 0.499 (10\%) and 0.465 (25\%), and Swin-B from 0.526 to 0.488 (10\%) and 0.453 (25\%). The pattern is consistent with global self-attention (DeiT3) distributing learned features more uniformly across heads than hierarchical window attention (Swin), making DeiT3 more amenable to uniform head removal.

\paragraph{KD-distilled Swin-T is catastrophically sensitive to head pruning.}
Head pruning on the best Swin-T KD checkpoint ($\alpha{=}0.0$, rank-1 = 0.616) produces a catastrophic collapse: 10\% head removal drops accuracy to 0.403 ($-$0.213), and 25\% to 0.381 ($-$0.235). This is far worse than pruning the weaker Swin-T Baseline (0.534 $\to$ 0.500 at 10\%). Block dropping on the same KD checkpoint, however, retains most of the accuracy (0.583), so the collapse is specific to head removal. A plausible explanation is that pure feature distillation ($\alpha{=}0.0$) concentrates learned representations into fewer heads, making the remaining heads critical---and therefore highly sensitive to removal. The implication for practitioners combining KD with pruning is that the distillation method and the pruning strategy must be co-designed.

\paragraph{Block dropping on Swin-B confirms depth redundancy.}
Removing 5 of the 18 blocks in Swin-Base's third stage increases rank-1 from 0.526 to 0.554 ($+$0.028) at INT8. The earlier hybrid-LoRA source likewise improves from 0.536 to 0.572 after block dropping and recovery fine-tuning. Both observed checkpoints therefore support depth reduction for this $96\times96$ task, although the experiment does not isolate the effects of block removal from subsequent fine-tuning.

\paragraph{INT8 quantization remains stable on pruned models.}
Across all 24 pruned models, INT8 introduces a mean absolute rank-1 change of 0.0017 and a median absolute change of 0.0013 relative to each pruned FP32 model. The largest drop is 0.0099 for DeiT3-S with 25\% head pruning (0.5515 to 0.5416); the largest increase is 0.0014. FP16 changes rank-1 by at most 0.0008. Quantization therefore adds a small accuracy change in most cases, but the unpruned four-model loss bound of 0.0016 does not apply to every pruned model.

\paragraph{Compression and accuracy.}
Among the pruned INT8 configurations, DeiT3-B KD ($\alpha{=}0.1$) with 10\% head pruning at INT8 achieves 0.591 rank-1 at 82\,MB ($4.0\times$ compression), preserving nearly all accuracy. For small models, Swin-T KD ($\alpha{=}0.0$) with 2 blocks removed at INT8 delivers 0.583 rank-1 at just 24.6\,MB---retaining 94.6\% of the unpruned KD model's accuracy in a package suitable for edge devices. For maximum compression with competitive accuracy, Swin-B with 5 blocks removed at INT8 delivers 0.554 rank-1 at 71.2\,MB ($4.7\times$), outperforming the unpruned Swin-B baseline. DeiT3-S at 10\% heads, INT8 achieves 0.565 rank-1 at 21.7\,MB ($3.8\times$), the smallest model that exceeds its unpruned counterpart. Relative to pruned FP32, the maximum absolute FP16 change is 0.0008 and the maximum INT8 loss is 0.0099. These file-size reductions should be considered alongside each configuration's accuracy and runtime support.

\subsection{On-Device Inference and TensorRT Compatibility}\label{sec:trt-compat}

All trained models were deployed to an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (8\,GB, JetPack 6.4, TensorRT 10.3, CUDA 12.6, SM compute capability 8.7) for hardware-in-the-loop latency evaluation. Inference is performed at batch size 1 with fixed $96\times96$ input resolution, reflecting a realistic single-image query scenario in a surveillance or access-control system.

\paragraph{Initial TRT Conversion Outcome.}
Of the sixteen baseline architectures, nine---the Swin and DeiT-3 families and the ResNet-D baselines---converted directly from opset-18 ONNX to native TensorRT FP32 and FP16 engines using \texttt{trtexec}. The remaining seven---ConvNeXt-T/S/B, PVT-v2-B2/B3/B5, and MobileViT-v2-200---failed standalone conversion with a \texttt{ForeignNode}/no-implementation error rooted in a normalization-layout mismatch (analyzed below). A per-family graph-rewrite procedure (Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat-success}) ultimately allowed all sixteen architectures to execute as native TensorRT engines with cosine similarity $\geq 0.999$ relative to the original PyTorch outputs. \paragraph{Architecturally Incompatible Models.}
The seven failing architectures all aborted \texttt{trtexec} conversion with the error:

\begin{quote}
\texttt{Could not find any implementation for node}\\
\{\texttt{ForeignNode[Transpose $\cdots$ Transpose]}\}.
\end{quote}

The root cause is a normalization pattern unique to these architectures. In standard ViTs like Swin and DeiT-3, layer normalization is applied to $(B,N,C)$ token sequences, which maps naturally to TensorRT's native \texttt{LayerNormalization} kernel along the channel axis. ConvNeXt and PVT-v2, however, apply layer normalization to NCHW spatial feature maps. In timm's implementation, this is realized by permuting the tensor to NHWC format, applying \texttt{F.layer\_norm}, then permuting back: \texttt{Transpose(0,2,3,1) $\rightarrow$ LayerNorm $\rightarrow$ Transpose(0,3,1,2)}. MobileViT-v2 uses an analogous \texttt{GroupNorm(num\_groups=1)}, which normalizes across all non-batch spatial dimensions and incurs the same layout issues.

TensorRT 10.3 on SM 8.7 contains no kernel that implements \texttt{LayerNormalization} on 4-D NCHW feature maps in either FP32 or FP16. The entire Transpose + normalize + Transpose pattern is fused by TRT's Myelin compiler backend into a placeholder \texttt{ForeignNode} for which no device implementation exists, causing conversion to abort.

\paragraph{A Practitioner's Guide to the Fix.}\label{sec:trt-compat-success}
The pitfalls below were identified in the course of getting the seven failing architectures to compile, and they generalise to any modern ConvNet--Transformer hybrid that is not specifically targeted at TensorRT~10.x on SM~8.7. The takeaway, which we elaborate in the rest of this paragraph, is that the only reliable fix is a per-family module-level rewrite that presents TensorRT with the supported $(B,N,C)$ \texttt{LayerNormalization} pattern. Workarounds that operate purely at the ONNX or opset level do not work on this hardware.

\paragraph{Opset changes and decomposition.} Lowering the export opset so that \texttt{LayerNormalization} is decomposed into its constituent arithmetic (ReduceMean, Sub, Pow, Sqrt, Div, Mul, Add) seems like it should remove the offending op, but TensorRT's Myelin compiler re-fuses the entire arithmetic subgraph back into the same unsupported \texttt{ForeignNode}. Conversely, exporting at the higher opset~17 with the native \texttt{LayerNormalization} op preserves the surrounding \texttt{Transpose $\rightarrow$ LayerNorm $\rightarrow$ Transpose} pattern, which TensorRT also has no kernel for in either FP32 or FP16. The diagnosis is the same in both cases: there is no SM~8.7 kernel for any non-$(B,N,C)$ LayerNorm, regardless of how the graph spells it.

\paragraph{Watch the dynamo exporter on TensorRT~10.x.} PyTorch's dynamo-based ONNX exporter rewrites the normalization to operate on a reshaped 3-D tensor, which is exactly what TensorRT wants, but in the process it inserts \texttt{SequenceEmpty} and \texttt{Loop} operators (from dynamic-shape handling) that TensorRT~10.3 cannot parse, so the engine build aborts before optimisation begins. The legacy TorchScript exporter at opset~17 is generally the safer default for TensorRT targets.

\paragraph{Half-measures at the model level are not enough.} Two intermediate fixes are tempting and both fall short. First, timm's ConvNeXt offers a \texttt{conv\_mlp=True} variant that replaces the MLP \texttt{nn.Linear} layers with $1\!\times\!1$ convolutions, eliminating the MLP-related transposes; but the implementation still routes through \texttt{LayerNorm2d} internally, so the normalization transposes survive. Second, a custom NCHW LayerNorm that normalises directly along the channel axis without any transpose still produces a ReduceMean chain that Myelin fuses into the same \texttt{ForeignNode}, confirming that the limitation is not graph structure but a missing kernel.

\paragraph{Post-hoc ONNX graph surgery is fragile.} Tools like \texttt{onnx-graphsurgeon} can in principle pattern-match the decomposed normalization and re-fuse it into a TRT-friendly form, but the approach is exposed to version skew between \texttt{onnx-graphsurgeon} and the installed \texttt{onnx} library; even when the rewrite itself is conceptually correct, an upstream-package mismatch can stop it from running at all.

\paragraph{The fix that does work is a per-family module-level rewrite.} The pattern is the same for all three families: replace the offending normalization with one that, after export, presents TensorRT with a $(B,N,C)$ \texttt{LayerNormalization} (or with elementwise ops that Myelin will not bundle into a foreign node), and verify numerical equivalence to the original PyTorch model on a fixed batch. For ConvNeXt this means swapping \texttt{LayerNorm2d} for a wrapper that explicitly reshapes $(B,C,H,W)\!\rightarrow\!(B,HW,C)$, applies the standard \texttt{nn.LayerNorm} along the channel axis, and reshapes back, while also using the \texttt{conv\_mlp=True} backbone to keep MLPs in NCHW. For PVT-v2 the model's fused-attention path is disabled before export, after which an opset-17 export already contains only $(B,N,C)$ normalizations. For MobileViT-v2 the single-group \texttt{GroupNorm} is replaced with a manual decomposition that computes the mean and variance over non-batch dimensions and applies the per-channel affine parameters as elementwise ops, sidestepping the missing GroupNorm kernel entirely.

\paragraph{Always verify cosine similarity end-to-end.} Each rewritten model should be checked against the original PyTorch implementation on a fixed batch, requiring cosine similarity $\geq 0.999$ between embeddings before any latency claim is reported. Without this check, a manually decomposed normalization can silently shift the embedding distribution and the regression will only appear in downstream identification metrics. All three rewrites used here pass this check and produce \texttt{trtexec}-compilable engines under TensorRT~10.3 on SM~8.7.

\paragraph{ONNX Runtime's TensorRT EP is a viable but slower fallback.} If a per-family rewrite is genuinely not feasible, the ONNX Runtime TensorRT Execution Provider will auto-partition the unrewritten ONNX graph and execute the unsupported sub-graphs on the CUDA EP. This works correctly but produces $1.18$--$2.66\times$ higher latencies than the rewritten native engines on the Jetson Orin Nano (e.g.\ ConvNeXt-T 4.53\,ms vs.\ 1.95\,ms FP16; MobileViT-v2-200 5.05\,ms vs.\ 1.90\,ms), because of sub-graph partitioning overhead and the unfused normalization fallback.

\paragraph{Key Findings and Implications for Practitioners.}

Not all vision backbones export to native TRT out of the box, but the gap is closable with principled graph rewrites. Models that apply layer normalization directly to NCHW spatial feature maps---rather than to flattened token sequences---fail standalone TRT 10.3 conversion on SM 8.7 because no kernel exists for that layout. The fix is not opset upgrades or operator decomposition (TRT's Myelin re-fuses both into the same unsupported ForeignNode), but module-level rewrites that present TRT with the supported $(B,N,C)$ \texttt{LayerNormalization} pattern while preserving numerical equivalence. ConvNeXt, PVT-v2 and MobileViT-v2---released in 2021--2022 and not specifically targeted at TRT 10.3---all fall in this category and all admit such rewrites.

The specific failure mode is a ForeignNode with tactic ID 0. When \texttt{trtexec} reports \texttt{Could not find any implementation for node} \{\texttt{ForeignNode[\ldots]}\} together with a warning about tactic 0x0 being skipped due to insufficient memory, the root cause is almost always a missing hardware kernel rather than a memory budget issue, and increasing \texttt{--memPoolSize} will not resolve it.

The ONNX Runtime TRT EP is a reasonable fallback when a per-family rewrite is not feasible, but it carries a measurable cost. The auto-partitioning EP achieves correct results without modifying the original ONNX graphs, at a $1.18$--$2.66\times$ latency penalty relative to the rewritten native engines on the Jetson Orin Nano (e.g.\ ConvNeXt-T 4.53\,ms vs.\ 1.95\,ms FP16, MobileViT-v2-200 5.05\,ms vs.\ 1.90\,ms). The penalty is attributable to sub-graph partitioning overhead and the unfused normalization fallback, both of which are eliminated by the rewritten engines.

Validating numerical equivalence is essential. For all three rewrite families, a fixed test batch was passed through both the original PyTorch model and the exported ONNX (and, where possible, the built TRT engine), and a cosine similarity $\geq 0.999$ was required. Without this check, manually-decomposed normalization layers risk silent accuracy loss that would only surface in downstream identification metrics.

FP16 precision provides a $1.53$--$2.02\times$ speedup over FP32 across all sixteen architectures, so FP16 is a sensible default to benchmark for embedded deployment.

ONNX Runtime dynamic INT8 did not provide a consistent GPU speedup. In the saved CUDA-EP benchmark, six of seven architectures ran $6.54$--$13.90\times$ slower than FP32: ConvNeXt-T increased from 5.98\,ms to 50.5\,ms and PVT-v2-B5 from 46.8\,ms to 306\,ms. MobileViT-v2-200 was the exception (19.3\,ms to 9.4\,ms). These timings do not isolate the contribution of integer-operator placement, fallback, or conversion overhead. Dynamic INT8 file compression should therefore not be treated as evidence of GPU acceleration. Native TensorRT calibration-based INT8 is a separate deployment path whose accuracy and latency must be checked independently.

\subsection{Per-Layer Bottleneck Analysis}\label{sec:layer-analysis}

To understand where inference time is spent within each architecture, TensorRT profiling was conducted on 10 representative FP16 models on the Jetson Orin Nano using \texttt{trtexec --dumpProfile}. Each layer's execution time was categorized into five groups: self-attention (QKV projections and softmax), FFN and linear layers (including fused MLP and standalone matrix multiplications), normalization (layer norm), convolutional and embedding layers (patch embedding, patch merging, downsampling convolutions), and other operations (reformatting, activation, pooling). Figure~\ref{fig:layer-breakdown} presents the results as a stacked percentage breakdown with absolute latency annotated.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{layer_breakdown.pdf}
    \caption{Per-layer time breakdown (FP16, Jetson Orin Nano) for DeiT-3, Swin, and ResNet models. Absolute latency is annotated above each bar. Self-attention (red) consumes a larger fraction in Swin models ($27$--$31\%$) than in DeiT-3 ($7$--$12\%$), while FFN and linear layers (blue) dominate DeiT-3 models ($68$--$83\%$).}
    \label{fig:layer-breakdown}
\end{figure}

Several architectural patterns are visible in the profiling data.

\paragraph{DeiT-3 is FFN-dominated.}
Across all three DeiT-3 model sizes, FFN and linear layers account for $68$--$83\%$ of total inference time, while self-attention contributes only $7$--$12\%$. The ratio shifts modestly with model size: DeiT3-Small allocates 12.1\% to attention, DeiT3-Medium 9.1\%, and DeiT3-Base 7.0\%. The near-constant absolute attention time ($\sim$0.2\,ms) across sizes reflects DeiT-3's fixed token count of 36 (from $96\times96$ input with $16\times16$ patches); attention compute is $O(N^2 d)$ where $N=36$ is small. The FFN, which scales with the hidden dimension $d_{\text{ff}} = 4d$, dominates because $d$ ranges from 384 (Small) to 768 (Base). FFN quantization and pruning therefore yield the largest latency benefits for DeiT-3 models, consistent with the observation that INT8 quantization---which primarily accelerates dense matrix multiplications---achieves its best latency improvements on DeiT-3.

\paragraph{Swin distributes compute more evenly between attention and FFN.}
Swin models allocate $27$--$31\%$ of time to self-attention, compared to DeiT-3's $7$--$12\%$, due to the windowed attention mechanism's processing of more tokens per window at each hierarchical stage. The attention fraction \emph{increases} slightly for smaller Swin models (Swin-Tiny: 30.3\%, Swin-Base: 26.8\%) because the FFN cost decreases faster with model width than the windowed attention overhead. The more balanced compute distribution in Swin is the main reason block dropping is effective for Swin: removing entire blocks eliminates both attention and FFN compute proportionally, whereas head pruning reduces only the attention portion---which accounts for less than a third of total time.

\paragraph{ResNet is conv-dominated.}
As expected, ResNet models spend $98$--$99\%$ of inference time in convolutional layers, with no attention or FFN components. This makes ResNet highly amenable to INT8 quantization via TRT's native PTQ: INT8 ResNet-50 achieves 0.63\,ms (vs.\ 0.95\,ms FP16, a $1.5\times$ speedup), while DeiT3-Small shows negligible INT8 latency improvement (1.05\,ms vs.\ 1.05\,ms). The INT8 benefit is concentrated in convolution-heavy operations, where TRT can exploit dedicated INT8 Tensor Cores.

\paragraph{Impact of pruning and KD on compute distribution.}
Comparing Swin-Tiny variants reveals that neither KD training nor block-level pruning substantially alters the \emph{percentage} breakdown: the KD-trained variant (CVLFace frozen) shows 30.5\% attention vs.\ 30.3\% for the baseline, and the 2-block-pruned variant shows 31.0\%. However, the absolute latencies differ meaningfully: the pruned model achieves 1.70\,ms total ($-25\%$ from unpruned 2.28\,ms), confirming that block dropping produces genuine latency savings that map directly to wall-clock improvements on hardware. The KD variant (1.93\,ms) is faster than the baseline (2.28\,ms) due to minor differences in attention pattern efficiency, though this effect may not generalize.

\subsection{Computational Profile across Resolutions}\label{sec:flops-profile}

The wall-time profile in Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat} captures \emph{what TensorRT actually executes on the Jetson Orin Nano}, but it conflates arithmetic intensity, kernel fusion quality and memory traffic. To separate these effects from the underlying architectural cost, we additionally measured pure inference arithmetic for each of the 16 architecture/size combinations evaluated in this chapter. We use \texttt{fvcore.nn.FlopCountAnalysis} (Detectron2's tracer-based counter) and report the multiply--add count, i.e.\ \textbf{GMACs}, throughout this subsection. Reported numbers are therefore half the FLOP count under the convention $1\,\text{MAC} = 2\,\text{FLOPs}$; we use ``GMACs'' as the single nomenclature in all axes, captions, tables and CSV columns to avoid the FLOPs-vs-MACs ambiguity that pervades the literature. fvcore attributes GMACs only to named modules; the residual softmax / scaled-dot-product BMM in self-attention is not module-bound and is not included in the per-module breakdown---this is benign at the resolutions evaluated here (the attention BMM contributes $<1\%$ of total at $96\times96$ for DeiT-3 with 36 tokens) but would dominate above $\sim$$512\times512$.
The sweep covers a power-of-two-ish resolution ladder $\{64, 96, 128, 160, 224, 256, 384, 512, 768, 1024\}$ for every family. timm's Swin v1 implementation pads the input internally so that the windowed attention partition is well-defined at any resolution; we therefore use the same ladder for it as for the other families. All measurements are taken at batch=1 with PyTorch eager mode on a workstation GPU (FP32) using \texttt{timm.create\_model(num\_classes=0)}, which strips the classification head so that the cost reflects the backbone alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:flops-vs-resolution} plots total GMACs against input resolution as a six-panel small-multiples view (one panel per architecture family, linear axes, shared y-range so cost can be compared directly across families).

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{flops_vs_resolution.pdf}
    \caption{Inference cost (GMACs, batch=1, FP32) versus input resolution for all 16 chapter architectures, grouped into per-family panels with a shared y-axis so that cost can be compared directly across families. Linear axes are used so that the super-linear ($\approx H^2$) growth is visible directly as a parabola; per-family panels prevent the four mid-size families from collapsing onto a single curve at the deployment resolutions. Counter: \texttt{fvcore.nn.FlopCountAnalysis}.}
    \label{fig:flops-vs-resolution}
\end{figure}

Two structural observations stand out. First, every family produces a clearly super-linear curve in resolution: GMACs grow roughly as $H^2$ because the dominant compute terms (FFN per token, $1\times1$ convolutions per pixel, $3\times3$ convolutions per pixel) all scale with the spatial token count, which itself is $O(H^2)$. Equivalently, on a pixel-axis the same data fits a slope $\approx 1.0$ within the swept range ($0.99$--$1.03$ across the six representatives), confirming the GMACs-linear-in-pixels regime. Second, neither global self-attention (DeiT-3) nor windowed attention (Swin) bends visibly above the per-family parabola within $[64, 1024]$\,px: the often-cited quadratic-in-tokens cost of global attention only becomes the dominant term well above the resolutions evaluated here, so model selection at $96\times96$ or $224\times224$ should not be driven by an asymptotic argument that is not yet active. The shared y-range also makes inter-family magnitude comparisons immediate: at $1024\times1024$ DeiT3-B and ConvNeXt-B trace nearly identical curves topping out at $\sim$$350$\,GMACs, ResNet-200d and Swin-B sit just below them ($\sim$$320$\,GMACs and $\sim$$340$\,GMACs), and MobileViTv2-200 occupies a different cost class entirely (peaking at $\sim$$115$\,GMACs).

Three architectural patterns emerge that cleanly explain---and in some cases contradict---the latency profile measured on the Jetson:

\paragraph{DeiT-3 and Swin are FFN-bound at $96\times96$.}
For all six DeiT-3 / Swin variants the FFN consumes $59$--$66\%$ of inference GMACs and the QKV $+$ output projections together consume an additional $30$--$33\%$. The dot-product attention itself is invisible at this resolution (DeiT-3 has only $36$ tokens, Swin partitions into $7\times7=49$-token windows). This is exactly the GMACs analogue of the wall-time finding from Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat} that DeiT-3 spends $68$--$83\%$ of its time in fused FFN/linear kernels. The agreement is not trivial: it confirms that on DeiT-3 the kernel fusion is good enough that GMACs share and time share track each other, which is why FFN quantization and head pruning work as expected. The Swin numbers also agree: Swin spends a higher latency fraction in the attention kernel ($27$--$31\%$) than its GMACs share would suggest ($\sim$$33\%$ for QKV+proj), because the window-partition / window-reverse memory shuffles around windowed attention add wall-time without adding GMACs.

Figure~\ref{fig:flops-breakdown-96} shows the per-module breakdown at the chapter's deployment resolution of $96\times96$, with absolute GMACs annotated at the right edge of each bar.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{flops_breakdown_at_96.pdf}
    \caption{Per-module GMACs breakdown for the 16 chapter architectures at the $96\times96$ TinyFace deployment resolution. Bars are normalised so that segments express the share of inference GMACs; absolute totals are annotated at the right edge. ConvNeXt's ``FFN/MLP'' segment is the inverted-bottleneck $1\times1$ pointwise stack, which the timm implementation registers as \texttt{nn.Linear}.}

\paragraph{ConvNeXt is dominated by $1\times1$ pointwise convolutions, not by depthwise.}
The timm ConvNeXt implementation expresses the inverted-bottleneck $1\times1$ convolutions as \texttt{nn.Linear} layers under \texttt{block.mlp.fc1} and \texttt{block.mlp.fc2}, so they are bucketed as \emph{FFN/MLP} in Figure~\ref{fig:flops-breakdown-96}. Under that bookkeeping the ``MLP'' segment contributes $93$--$96\%$ of ConvNeXt's GMACs and the named \texttt{conv\_dw} depthwise $7\times7$ contributes only $1$--$2\%$. Translated back to the convolutional view, the inverted bottleneck (which is just the $1\times1$ projection up to $4\times$ width and back) accounts for almost the entire GMACs budget and the $7\times7$ depthwise is a near-free spatial mixer. The $96\times96$ GMACs cost is modest ($0.82$\,GMACs for ConvNeXt-T), and after the per-family graph rewrite documented in Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat} the resulting native TRT engine runs at $1.95$\,ms FP16 on the Jetson Orin Nano---in the same band as DeiT3-M ($1.92$\,ms) and only $17\%$ behind the comparably sized Swin-T ($1.67$\,ms). The earlier wall-time penalty often attributed to the un-fused \texttt{Transpose$\to$LayerNorm$\to$Transpose} pattern was a property of the unrewritten ONNX graph (and of ONNX Runtime TRT EP, which falls back to the CUDA EP for that subgraph), not an inherent architectural cost of ConvNeXt itself.

\paragraph{PVT v2 and MobileViT v2 are hybrid.}
PVT v2's spatial-reduction attention partly suppresses the QKV cost at deep stages, so the family still spends $68$--$72\%$ of GMACs on the FFN but only $\sim$$10$--$13\%$ on QKV (vs.\ $\sim$$25\%$ for DeiT-3 and Swin); the spatial-reduction $1\times1$/strided convs end up in the \emph{Attn other} category and account for a non-trivial $\sim$$5\%$. MobileViT v2 is the most distinctive of the 16: convolutional layers (the MobileNet-v2-like stem and the depthwise/pointwise mobile blocks) account for $60\%$ of total GMACs, with separable transformer blocks splitting the remainder between FFN ($20\%$) and the linear projections inside the separable attention ($15\%$). This dual-budget profile is the architectural reason MobileViT v2 places so differently on the Pareto curve from both pure CNNs and pure transformers.

\paragraph{ResNet is conv-bound, as expected.}
ResNet-D variants spend $91$--$97\%$ of GMACs in the bottleneck convolutions, with the strided $1\times1$ downsample shortcuts contributing the small remainder. The conv share \emph{rises} with depth (50d $\to$ 200d) because the additional bottleneck stages add convolutions without adding downsample shortcuts. This matches the wall-time profile ($98$--$99\%$ in conv kernels) almost exactly; ResNet-D is the family for which fvcore-measured GMACs are the cleanest predictor of measured Jetson latency.

The experimental findings carry several implications for embedded vision transformer deployment:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compact transformers suit low-resolution inputs.} Swin-Tiny (27.5M) outperforms ResNet-200 (62.6M) by $+$0.066 rank-1 in the baseline setting, and by $+$0.148 with optimized KD. On TinyFace, where input resolution is severely limited, transformers offer a favorable accuracy--efficiency trade-off. Self-attention's global receptive field is a likely source of this advantage, since it lets the model exploit limited spatial information more effectively than local convolutions; the relative benefit may differ on higher-resolution or non-biometric tasks.

    \item \textbf{LoRA with progressive unfreezing has architecture-dependent outcomes.} On TinyFace, this hybrid improved all Swin variants and degraded all DeiT-3 variants relative to their no-adapter progressive-unfreezing baselines. The schedules ultimately trained approximately 96--98\% of original Swin backbone parameters and 66\% of original DeiT-3 parameters, although selected checkpoints can come from earlier epochs. Standard frozen-backbone LoRA was also completed in the preliminary sweeps; its EVAL metrics are distinct from these test-set results. A common-split comparison with matched hyperparameters is needed to separate the contributions of adapters, unfreezing, and architecture.

    \item \textbf{Knowledge distillation requires careful tuning of the loss mixing coefficient.} The commonly used $\alpha = 0.7$ leaves significant accuracy on the table. With proper tuning, KD boosts Swin-Tiny from 0.534 (baseline) to 0.616 (a 15.4\% relative improvement), making it competitive with models three times its size.

    \item \textbf{Teacher accuracy on the target task interacts non-trivially with the mixing coefficient.} The higher-accuracy CVLFace teacher enabled pure feature distillation ($\alpha = 0.0$), while the lower-accuracy frozen PETALface teacher required hard-loss anchoring ($\alpha \geq 0.1$) to prevent training collapse. KD configurations should be validated end-to-end rather than relying on teacher accuracy as a proxy for student improvement, and the optimal $\alpha$ should be re-tuned when changing teachers or target datasets.

    \item \textbf{Quantization offers substantial file-size savings with small accuracy changes.} The four unpruned examples lose at most 0.0016 rank-1 under dynamic INT8, with $3.8$--$3.9\times$ smaller files. Across 24 pruned models, the maximum additional INT8 loss is 0.0099; FP16 changes rank-1 by at most 0.0008. Native TensorRT FP16 is the measured GPU default here. Dynamic INT8 storage and native GPU INT8 acceleration require separate validation.

    \item \textbf{Structured pruning is architecture- and strategy-dependent.} Block dropping improved both Swin variants (Swin-T $+$0.010, Swin-B $+$0.028 rank-1 from baseline) and was the only safe pruning method for KD-distilled Swin-T, while head pruning degraded Swin at every ratio tested. For DeiT3 models, conservative head pruning (10\%) improved DeiT3-S ($+$0.013 rank-1) and preserved DeiT3-B KD accuracy (0.591 $\approx$ 0.593). The pruning axis (depth vs.\ width) needs to be matched to both the architecture and the training regime, and automated pruning search may unlock further gains.

    \item \textbf{KD and pruning interact in non-obvious ways.} The strongest KD checkpoint (Swin-T, $\alpha{=}0.0$, rank-1 = 0.616) suffers catastrophic degradation under head pruning ($-$0.213 at 10\%) even though the same pruning is benign on the weaker baseline (0.534 $\to$ 0.500). Distillation with $\alpha{=}0.0$ (pure feature matching) appears to concentrate knowledge into fewer heads, which makes the model fragile under head removal. Practitioners planning to combine KD with pruning should either use moderate $\alpha$ values or prefer block dropping to preserve distilled representations.

    \item \textbf{Pruning and quantization can be combined, with checkpoint-specific accuracy checks.} The median absolute INT8 change across pruned models is 0.0013, but the largest drop is 0.0099. Selected pruning$+$INT8 configurations achieve $3.8$--$5.1\times$ file-size compression while maintaining or improving rank-1 over their unpruned FP32 sources; this is not a guarantee for every pruning setting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Power-Mode Trade-offs}\label{sec:power-modes}

All latency numbers reported above were measured under the Jetson Orin Nano's \texttt{MAXN\_SUPER} power mode, which removes the on-board wattage cap. Practical embedded deployments, however, often run under a tighter thermal or battery budget. To quantify how the previously reported throughput scales when this budget is reduced, we re-benchmarked a representative subset of fourteen (model, precision) configurations across the three power modes exposed by \texttt{nvpmodel} on the Orin Nano ``Super'' firmware (R36.4.3): \textbf{15W} (ID 0), \textbf{25W} (ID 1) and \textbf{MAXN\_SUPER} (ID 2). Between modes we issued \texttt{nvpmodel -m <id>} followed by \texttt{jetson\_clocks} and a five-second settling sleep so that DVFS, fan and voltage rails reached steady state before the timing window opened; all other \texttt{trtexec} arguments (workspace, warmup, iterations, CUDA-graph capture, batch size) were held identical so that the only varying factor is the runtime power cap. All fourteen configurations---including ConvNeXt-T and PVTv2-B2, which were re-exported using the per-family rewrites of Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat-success}---were benchmarked through the same unified native-TRT harness.

Input-rail power was sampled throughout each mode sweep with \texttt{tegrastats --interval 500} and parsed for the \texttt{VDD\_IN} rail. These pooled measurements cover the full model sweep rather than an individual model: the 15W mode draws $7.6$\,W mean / $13.0$\,W p95, the 25W mode draws $12.4$\,W mean / $15.0$\,W p95, and \texttt{MAXN\_SUPER} draws $13.5$\,W mean / $16.0$\,W p95. The pooled mean rises by about $1.1$\,W from 25W to \texttt{MAXN\_SUPER}. These workload-specific means and p95 values do not establish a per-model maximum draw or a guaranteed thermal envelope.

\paragraph{Discussion.} The sweep results break down into three patterns:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graceful degradation under 15W.} The throughput coefficient of variation across the three modes (\textit{FPS CV} column in Table~\ref{tab:power-modes}) stays in a narrow $0.096$--$0.171$ range: no model ``collapses'' under the 15W cap. The smallest variance ($\sim$0.10) is observed for the DeiT3-B family (DeiT3-B baseline, KD~$\alpha{=}0.1$ and head-pruned baseline), making them the most ``power-mode-stable'' configurations in the suite. This is consistent with the per-layer profiling of Section~\ref{sec:layer-analysis}: DeiT3-B's runtime is dominated by FFN GEMMs that already saturate the GPU SMs at the 15W clock cap, leaving little additional throughput to extract at higher clocks.
    \item \textbf{Lightweight workloads scale most aggressively with the budget.} The largest 15W slowdowns are seen on PVTv2-B2 ($1.40\times$), ConvNeXt-T ($1.33\times$), DeiT3-S ($1.32\times$), Swin-T-KD-blocks~2 ($1.30\times$) and Swin-T-KD~$\alpha{=}0$ ($1.30\times$): exactly the models whose per-iteration kernels are short enough to be limited by GPU clock rather than by SM occupancy. PVTv2-B2's especially clock-sensitive behaviour is consistent with its layer-norm-heavy graph, even after the rewrite to native TRT.
    \item \textbf{Configured budget and measured draw differ.} The 15W setting gives a pooled mean of $7.6$\,W and p95 of $13.0$\,W for this sweep. The 25W setting retains $94$--$98\%$ of \texttt{MAXN\_SUPER} throughput across the fourteen configurations at about $1.1$\,W lower pooled mean draw. The mode label alone therefore cannot predict application power consumption.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deployment recommendation per power budget.} The 15W setting is a useful candidate when power is constrained: Swin-T KD with two blocks removed runs at $1.87$\,ms (534\,FPS), versus $2.15$\,ms (464\,FPS) for the higher-accuracy unpruned KD model. The pooled $7.6$\,W mean cannot establish that either model meets a strict 10\,W ceiling, especially with a pooled p95 of $13.0$\,W. The 25W setting retains most measured throughput with lower pooled draw; \texttt{MAXN\_SUPER} gives the highest throughput in this sweep. Select among them using the application's latency, peak-power, and thermal measurements.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter investigated the full design space for deploying vision transformers on resource-constrained embedded systems, using face identification on the TinyFace benchmark~\cite{cheng2018lowresface} as a case study. Starting from 16 model variants across six architecture families, the study systematically evaluated standard LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} sweeps and LoRA combined with progressive backbone unfreezing, feature-level knowledge distillation with two teacher configurations, structured pruning (head removal and block dropping), and post-training quantization (FP16, INT8)---producing over 100 model configurations that were benchmarked for both biometric accuracy and on-device inference latency on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano. The resulting analysis connects architectural design choices to measurable deployment outcomes and identifies several findings with practical implications for embedded vision systems.

The clearest finding is that on this low-resolution task, architecture choice dominates the accuracy--efficiency trade-off. Compact vision transformers consistently outperform larger CNNs: Swin-Tiny (27.5M parameters) exceeds ResNet-200 (62.6M) by $+$0.066 rank-1 in the baseline setting, and with optimally tuned knowledge distillation ($\alpha = 0.0$, frozen CVLFace teacher), Swin-Tiny reaches 0.616 rank-1---an absolute gain of $+$0.082 over its baseline (a 15.4\% relative improvement) that makes a 27.5M-parameter model competitive with unpruned 86M-parameter alternatives (Table~\ref{tab:overall-summary}). Self-attention's global receptive field appears to be a key factor when discriminative features must be extracted from severely resolution-limited inputs ($96\times96$ from $\sim$30$\times$30 native). The combined LoRA-and-unfreezing protocol improved all Swin variants ($+$0.010 to $+$0.028 absolute rank-1) while degrading all DeiT-3 variants ($-$0.025 to $-$0.037); these results do not isolate the effect of standard frozen-backbone LoRA. Head pruning was well-tolerated by DeiT-3 but catastrophic for KD-distilled Swin models. These architecture-dependent interactions need to be tested per family.

Model compression proved effective when matched to the architecture. For four representative unpruned models, dynamic INT8 loses at most 0.0016 rank-1 with $3.8$--$3.9\times$ file-size reduction. Across 24 pruned models, its mean and median absolute changes are 0.0017 and 0.0013, with a maximum loss of 0.0099 relative to pruned FP32. FP16 is near-lossless, with at most 0.0008 absolute rank-1 change across those pruned models. After recovery fine-tuning, block dropping improves Swin-T ($+$0.010) and Swin-B ($+$0.028) at INT8 over their unpruned FP32 baselines; 10\% head pruning improves DeiT3-S ($+$0.013 to 0.565). Selected pruning-and-INT8 configurations deliver $3.8$--$5.1\times$ file-size compression (Table~\ref{tab:pruning-results}), but each requires an accuracy check. The strongest Swin-T KD checkpoint (0.616) falls to 0.403 after 10\% head pruning, while block dropping retains 0.583. This suggests an interaction between distillation and pruning; concentration of features into fewer heads remains a hypothesis rather than a measured mechanism.

Hardware-in-the-loop evaluation on the Jetson Orin Nano showed that native TensorRT compatibility is architecture-dependent but, for every architecture in this study, recoverable through a principled module-level graph rewrite. Models that apply layer normalization to NCHW spatial feature maps---ConvNeXt, PVT-v2, and MobileViT-v2---initially failed \texttt{trtexec} conversion entirely, due to missing SM 8.7 kernels for 4-D normalization patterns. Several intuitive workarounds (opset decomposition, a custom NCHW normalization module, post-hoc ONNX graph surgery) all fail because TensorRT's Myelin compiler re-fuses the rewritten arithmetic back into the same unsupported \texttt{ForeignNode}. The fix that does work is per-family and module-level: ConvNeXt's \texttt{LayerNorm2d} is replaced with a wrapper that reshapes to $(B,HW,C)$ around a standard \texttt{nn.LayerNorm}, combined with timm's \texttt{conv\_mlp=True} backbone; PVT-v2's fused-attention path is disabled before export to expose a clean opset-17 graph; and MobileViT-v2's single-group \texttt{GroupNorm} is replaced by a manual elementwise decomposition. All three rewrites reproduce the original PyTorch outputs to cosine similarity $\geq 0.999$ and produce \texttt{trtexec}-compilable engines, allowing all sixteen architectures to be benchmarked under a single uniform harness. Under that harness the previously reported $2.63\times$ Swin-T vs.\ ConvNeXt-T gap (1.72\,ms vs.\ 4.53\,ms FP16) shrinks to $1.17\times$ (1.67\,ms vs.\ 1.95\,ms), and the ONNX Runtime TensorRT EP fallback---a viable workaround when a per-family rewrite is not feasible---incurs a measurable $1.18$--$2.66\times$ latency penalty relative to the rewritten native engines (e.g.\ ConvNeXt-T 4.53\,ms vs.\ 1.95\,ms FP16, MobileViT-v2-200 5.05\,ms vs.\ 1.90\,ms). Per-layer profiling (Figure~\ref{fig:layer-breakdown}) further showed that DeiT-3 models are FFN-dominated ($68$--$83\%$ of compute), Swin models distribute compute more evenly between attention and FFN ($27$--$31\%$ attention), and ResNet is almost entirely convolution-bound ($98$--$99\%$). These profiles explain why block dropping is effective for Swin (it removes both attention and FFN proportionally) while head pruning has limited latency impact (attention accounts for less than a third of total time).

Several practical challenges shaped the experimental design. Training with the AdaFace margin-based loss~\cite{narayan2025petalface} required careful hyperparameter tuning per architecture family to avoid numerical instability, particularly for larger models. The extreme input resolution mismatch between TinyFace's native $\sim$30$\times$30 imagery and the models' expected input size meant that all models operated on heavily interpolated inputs, placing a ceiling on achievable accuracy regardless of model capacity. The Jetson Orin Nano's 8\,GB unified memory constrained batch sizes during evaluation and made reduced precision useful for larger models, reinforcing the practical importance of the quantization and pruning pipeline developed in this work.

For practitioners, Swin-T KD ($\alpha{=}0.0$, frozen CVLFace teacher) has the highest observed student accuracy, 0.616 rank-1, at 1.34\,ms native FP16 latency in the deployment comparison. Dropping two blocks gives 0.583 rank-1, 1.17\,ms and a 48.6\,MiB engine. Pruned DeiT3-S FP16 offers 0.564 rank-1 at 1.02\,ms in a 42.6\,MiB engine. Its calibrated INT8 engine reduces storage and RAM demand, but is slower, less accurate, and uses more energy per inference. Table~\ref{tab:deployment-guide} compares these native choices. Validate TRT compatibility early; the per-family rewrites in Section~\ref{sec:trt-compat-success} restore the native path for seven initially failing architectures, with embedding cosine similarity $\geq 0.999$ to the original models.

Several directions remain for future work. Joint knowledge distillation and pruning---optimizing both simultaneously rather than sequentially---may yield further improvements and potentially avoid the fragility observed when pruning KD-distilled models. Automated pruning search across both depth and width axes simultaneously could identify configurations that manual grid search misses~\cite{Park2023PTPrune}. Extending this analysis to additional edge platforms (Qualcomm Hexagon DSP, Apple Neural Engine, MediaTek APU) would reveal how architecture rankings change with hardware-specific kernel support~\cite{Ngo2025EdgeInference}. Newer efficient transformer architectures such as EfficientViT~\cite{liu2023efficientvit} and FastViT~\cite{vasu2023fastvit}, which incorporate hardware-aware design principles from their inception, may shift the Pareto front established here. Finally, evaluating these findings on higher-resolution biometric tasks and non-biometric vision domains would help establish the generality of the architecture-dependent optimization patterns observed on TinyFace.

The broader picture is that vision transformers are now a practical option for embedded deployment, not just a research subject. The relevant question is no longer whether transformers can run on edge devices, but how to navigate the design space of architectures, training regimes, and compression techniques to find configurations that meet specific deployment constraints. The architecture-aware methodology presented in this chapter is intended as a starting template for that process, and we expect it to remain useful as both transformer architectures and embedded hardware continue to evolve.
